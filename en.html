Foundations – what stands above is decided below

In your studies so far you have learnt the basics of soil mechanics and the design and construction of earthworks. In this course we turn all of that into a single question: how do we transfer the loads of a structure to the ground without anything being damaged?

In engineering terms, soil is the outermost, loose cover layer of the Earth’s crust. We build our engineering structures on it, it governs their foundations and their behaviour, and earthworks are made from it. In Hungary this layer typically consists of loose sediments formed from the broken-down, weathered grains of solid rock.

Soil is therefore itself a rock – a sedimentary rock – but its bearing resistance and deformation properties are far less favourable than those of solid rock. Across most of Hungary the near-surface ground is soil rather than solid rock, which is why soil mechanics carries much more weight here than rock mechanics.

From rock to soil

Soil–structure interaction

try it

Every structure is connected to the Earth through its foundation. The structure induces stresses in the ground, the stresses cause deformation – and this acts back on the structure, possibly even on its stability. As the ground deforms, the structure deforms with it, and the stresses within the structure are redistributed.

Soil is a multiphase medium: solid grains, water and air. Under load it compresses and the building settles – often very slowly, depending on the permeability of the soil (this is consolidation). Settlements under buildings are typically of the order of centimetres, and they are not uniform.

Differential settlement induces forces and stresses in the superstructure: the structure may crack and, in extreme cases – through overloading – the ground beneath the foundation may even fail. Try it on the model: see what a soft lens under the building does!

The reinforced concrete raft can bend, but its stiffness makes it curve smoothly, without breaking: even on uniform ground its centre settles most, and the contact pressure is higher at its edges. The differential settlement (Δs) is the difference between the largest and the smallest settlement. The angular distortion (β) is the differential settlement (δs) of two adjacent walls or columns (here every 3.5 m) divided by their spacing (ℓ), after removing the tilt of the whole building – the coloured segment in the figure shows where it is largest. This, not the average settlement, is what cracks the walls.

That is why foundation design starts with getting to know the ground: without a good ground investigation there is no safe and economical foundation.

The purpose of a foundation

To transfer the loads of the superstructure to the ground without causing damage to the structure – cracking, excessive tilt or bending.

There are many foundation systems, but they fall into two main groups: shallow foundations and deep foundations. There are also mixed (combined, hybrid) foundations. Switch between the types and see where the foundation transfers the load!

Why are foundations more sensitive than other structures?

1Hard to standardise

The same building meets different ground conditions on every site – the foundation always has to be thought through again.

2Built below ground level

It is built in worse conditions, construction accuracy is much harder to maintain, and the finished structure cannot be seen.

3Its defects endanger the whole building

It can be repaired afterwards only with great difficulty and at great cost, and often even the cause of the defect is hard to establish.

And why is it getting harder?
In towns and cities most of the land with good ground has already been built on. What is left is often marshy, made ground, undermined by mining or prone to landslides.
Structures are becoming ever more sensitive: longer spans, widely varying numbers of storeys, and ever closer to neighbouring buildings.
